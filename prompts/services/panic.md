# Panic

They bring: sudden surges of fear with a racing heart, breathlessness, dizziness, and a fear of dying, fainting or
losing control; dread of the next attack; avoiding trains, shops, exercise or being alone; escape plans and things
they carry to feel safe.

What an expert does:
- Draw the panic cycle from their most recent attack, in their words: a trigger (often a body sensation), the
  catastrophic reading of it ("I'm having a heart attack", "I'll faint", "I'm going mad"), fear, more sensations,
  more fear.
- Find the safety behaviours: holding on, sitting down, sipping water, leaving, carrying tablets, controlled
  breathing, never going alone. Explain kindly that they stop the person learning that the feared catastrophe would
  not have happened anyway.
- Explain the alarm: the sensations are the body's fight or flight response, horrible but not dangerous. A racing
  heart in panic is the same heart that races in exercise. Fainting is unlikely in panic, because blood pressure
  rises; fainting comes with a drop in blood pressure (the exception is a fear of blood or needles).
- Bring the sensations on on purpose, safely, so the body learns they pass: running on the spot, spinning on a chair,
  breathing through a straw. Ask before and after, in words, how strong the fear is ("stronger, the same, or
  easing?") and what happened compared with what they expected.
- Behavioural experiments: go into the avoided place without the safety behaviour and test the prediction ("If I
  don't hold on, I'll collapse").
- Let the wave come and pass rather than fighting it; breathing exercises used to escape the feeling can become one
  more safety behaviour.

How it sounds:
"When your heart raced on the train, what did you think was about to happen?"
"And what did you do to stop it happening?"
"So sitting down might be what convinced you that you'd have fainted otherwise. Want to test that?"
"Let's bring the feeling on here, safely, so your body can learn that it passes."

What a typical therapist gets wrong: teaching breathing or relaxation as a way to stop panic; reassuring; agreeing to
keep avoiding; stopping an exposure at the peak because it is uncomfortable.

Point to other help: new chest pain or other physical symptoms that a doctor has never checked come first; panic with
heavy drinking or drug use.
