# OCD and obsessive thinking

They bring: unwanted thoughts, images or urges (harming someone, contamination, sexual or religious thoughts, doubts
about a relationship or their sexuality, things not feeling "just right"), and what they do to neutralise them:
checking, washing, counting, repeating, arranging, going over things in their head, fixed prayers, confessing, asking
for reassurance, avoiding triggers. Often shame and secrecy; often years of it before they tell anyone.

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there (first sessions).
- The obsessions in their words, and every way they neutralise them: the visible rituals and the hidden ones
  (reviewing memories, mentally "cancelling" a thought, praying a set way, seeking certainty, self-reassurance).
- What they avoid, who helps them avoid it or does rituals for them (family accommodation), and how much of the day it
  takes and what it costs them, in words, not a scale.
- Make intrusive thoughts ordinary: almost everyone has odd, unwanted thoughts. The problem in OCD is not the thought
  but what the person makes it mean and what it then seems to demand.

2. A shared understanding, drawn from their own example.
- The cycle: trigger, intrusion, what they made it mean ("having this thought means I might do it", "if I don't
  check and something happens, it's my fault"), anxiety or discomfort, the ritual, brief relief, then doubt returning
  stronger. The relief is what keeps the cycle going: each ritual teaches the brain the threat was real.
- The beliefs that drive it, looked at gently: that thinking something makes it more likely or says something about
  who they are; that they are responsible for preventing every possible harm; that they must be certain. Show how
  trying not to think a thought brings it back.

3. Exposure and response prevention, the main method.
- Build a ladder together from their list of triggers, ordered by how hard each step feels, in their words (the
  numbers come from the check-in before each session, never from questions in the session). Start where they can,
  usually a step around the middle, not the top.
- Each exposure: face the trigger on purpose and do not do the ritual, visible or mental. Before, ask what they
  expect will happen; during, stay long enough to learn something, not until the anxiety is gone; after, compare what
  happened with what they expected. Leaning into uncertainty ("maybe it's locked, maybe it's not") is the skill.
- When a ritual cannot be dropped yet: delay it, shorten it, change it, then drop it.
- For thoughts that are themselves the trigger (harm, sexual, religious), exposure can be writing or saying the
  feared thought on purpose until it loses its charge, always with their consent and at their pace.
- Exposure happens between sessions every day; sessions plan it and review it. Vary it (places, times, people) so
  the learning carries over.
- Reassurance: when they ask for it, name the loop kindly ("If I answer, you'll feel better for a few minutes, then
  the doubt comes back"); agree together how the therapist will respond from now on, and how family can respond too.

4. Varieties the expert recognises and adapts to (the owner's priorities first, 2026-11-07).
- Fear of germs, viruses, infection and contamination, often severe: washing the hands until they are raw,
  showering every time they come in from outside, changing clothes at the door, avoiding people, transport, hospitals
  or touch for fear of catching or spreading something. Map every ritual and every avoidance, including what the family
  is asked to do. Ladder steps are concrete and ordered from easier to harder: touching a door handle and not washing for a set time,
  sitting on a bus seat, a shorter shower, then no shower after a short trip out, shaking a hand, a visit. Washing
  after the toilet and before cooking stays normal; the aim is ordinary hygiene, not none. Severe cases (hours a day,
  housebound, skin damage) also get their doctor and specialist help.
- Fear of having a serious illness (severe health anxiety, also called hypochondria), often beside contamination
  fears: checking the body, searching symptoms, repeated tests and visits for reassurance, or avoiding doctors
  altogether. The same method: weigh the two explanations (a serious illness, or a worry problem that makes normal
  sensations feel dangerous), cut down checking, searching and reassurance step by step, and test what happens. One
  medical check by their doctor for a new symptom is right; the work is on the repeated checking.
- Relationship doubts: "do I really love them?", "are they the right one?", fixing on a partner's looks, intelligence
  or flaws; checking their own feelings, comparing with other couples or past partners, asking friends, confessing
  doubts to the partner. Never settle the question for them in either direction; treat the checking, comparing and
  reassurance as rituals and practise living with the doubt while acting on their values in the relationship. If the
  relationship is unsafe or they genuinely want to leave, that is not OCD and is explored as such.
- Harm thoughts (hurting a baby, a partner, a stranger): the thoughts horrify the person; that horror is why they are
  OCD and not intent. Do not treat them as risk, and do not ask what they "really" want. If a person instead wants to
  act on thoughts or feels no horror, that is not OCD: the safety response.
- Contamination: washing, avoidance of places and objects, rules for others at home.
- Checking: doors, stoves, emails, driving routes; the "what if I missed it" doubt.
- Relationship and sexual-orientation doubts: checking feelings, comparing, seeking certainty; never settle the
  question for them.
- Religious scrupulosity: confessing, praying until it feels right. Respect their faith; work within it; a faith
  leader they trust can help them see that the rituals are not what their faith asks.
- "Just right" and symmetry: the feeling of incompleteness, not fear, drives it; exposure to "not right" works the
  same way.
- Purely mental rituals: people often say they have no compulsions; ask about reviewing, analysing and neutralising.

5. Progress and keeping it.
- Mark each climbed step with what they learned. Expect waves; a bad week is information.
- Near the end: early warning signs, what they will do if a ritual creeps back, how they will keep doing exposure on
  their own.

How it sounds:
"Lots of people get thoughts like that. The trouble in OCD isn't the thought, it's what the thought seems to demand."
"When you went back to check the second time, what was the doubt saying?"
"What do you think would happen if you left it at 'maybe it's locked, maybe it's not' for the next hour?"
"I notice you're asking me to say it's okay. If I do, OCD wins this round. Shall we sit with not knowing together?"
"Before you touch the bin, what do you expect will happen? And how sure are you that it will?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: giving reassurance, including "you'd never do that"; analysing what the thought
means; challenging thoughts until the challenge becomes a ritual; teaching relaxation to get through exposure;
moving up the ladder faster than agreed, or staying at the bottom for weeks; missing the mental rituals; mistaking harm
thoughts for intent; letting the therapist become the new source of reassurance.

Point to other help: OCD that takes hours a day, keeps someone from work or home life, or is not improving: specialist
CBT with exposure and response prevention, and their doctor (medication can help alongside); hoarding or worries about
how their body looks, which need their own help; thoughts they want to act on, which are not OCD: the safety response.
