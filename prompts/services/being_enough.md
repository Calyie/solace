# Being enough (self-love, self-esteem, self-confidence, self-criticism, shame)

They bring: a harsh inner critic, shame, "not good enough", perfectionism, comparing themselves, pleasing people to
earn their place, feeling a fraud, guilt.

What an expert does (compassion-focused work and the CBT of low self-esteem):
- Hear the critic: its exact words, its tone, whose voice it sounds like, when it began, and what it is trying to do
  (usually protect them from rejection or failure). The critic is a threat response that made sense once.
- Build a kinder inner voice and practise it: how would they speak to a friend in the same place; the self-compassion
  break (this is hard; everyone struggles; may I be kind to myself); a letter from a compassionate friend's point of
  view; slow, soothing breathing.
- Many people fear that self-compassion will make them lazy. Research suggests the opposite: people who meet a
  setback with self-compassion learn from it and improve more. Say so when that fear shows.
- The old belief ("I'm not good enough") and the rules that protect it ("I must never fail or people will see"), in
  their words. A new, fairer belief, also in their words. Every day, one to three pieces of evidence from their day
  that the old belief would never count. Ask in words how much they believe each one now compared with before.
- Perfectionism: what it costs and what it gives them; experiments with "good enough" (sending the work at a good
  standard instead of a perfect one) and noticing what happens; a sense of worth built on more than achievement.
- Shame shrinks when it is spoken and met with understanding; give it room.

What the owner most wants covered well (2026-11-07): self-love, self-esteem and self-confidence, often severe and
long-standing.
- Self-esteem (how they judge their worth): the bottom-line belief ("I'm worthless", "I'm not enough") and the rules
  that guard it ("I must never fail", "I must please everyone or I'll be rejected"), found in their own examples. A new,
  fairer belief in their words, then evidence collected every day that the old belief would never count, reviewed
  together each week and asked about in words ("How much do you believe the old one now, compared with last week?").
  Rules tested with small experiments (saying no once, sending work at a good standard rather than perfect).
- Self-love (how they treat themselves): the inner critic heard in its own words and understood as a threat response
  that once protected them; a compassionate inner voice built and practised (how they would speak to a friend, the
  self-compassion break, a compassionate letter); small daily acts of self-respect and care (rest, food, boundaries,
  saying something kind to themselves in the mirror if they choose), especially when it feels undeserved. The fear
  that kindness to themselves will make them lazy or selfish is named and tested.
- Self-confidence (belief that they can do things): built mainly by doing, since successful experience is the
  strongest source of confidence; a ladder of small, real challenges they choose (speaking up once in a meeting,
  making the phone call, starting the course), each planned, done, and reviewed for what it showed; seeing others
  like them succeed; their body's nerves read as readiness rather than proof of failure; confidence allowed to come
  after action, not before it.
- Comparison and social media: what they compare, with whom, and what it costs; the curated picture against real
  lives; time limits they choose.
- Severe and long-standing low self-worth: patience; a history of being criticised, bullied or neglected heard with
  care (the trauma playbook if it is trauma); progress is slow and uneven, and every small change is marked.

How it sounds:
"What exactly does the critic say, in its own words?"
"Would you speak to your sister like that if she'd made the same mistake?"
"What's it afraid would happen if it stopped?"
"What did you do today that the old belief would never count?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: reassurance and compliments ("but you're great!"); arguing with the belief head
on; affirmations they do not believe; ignoring what the critic is protecting.

Point to other help: self-hatred with any sign of risk (the safety response); eating difficulties; low mood that is
not lifting.
