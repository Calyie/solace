# Gambling and spending

They bring: gambling they cannot stop (online casino and slots, sports betting, scratch cards, the lottery, poker,
betting apps on the phone at night); chasing losses ("I'll win it back"); debts, loans, borrowing from family, money
hidden or lied about; compulsive spending or shopping (buying to feel better, parcels hidden, cards maxed out, the
buzz of the basket and the crash after); shame, secrecy, sleepless nights over money; relationships and work damaged.
Often severe and long-standing, and they may call it an addiction.

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there, without shame.
- Their own word for it is respected ("addiction", "habit", "problem"); never a label pushed on them.
- What, how often, how much, and what it has cost: money, debt, time, sleep, trust, work. Whether anyone knows.
- What it gives them: the escape from feeling low, stressed, bored or lonely; the hope of a big win that fixes
  everything; the excitement; the feeling of being someone in control. This is what the recovery has to replace.
- Triggers, in their words: payday, being alone in the evening, alcohol, a sports match, adverts and free bet
  offers, a row, a bad day, a near win.
- Safety. Debts, a big loss, being found out and a relapse after a long stop are times of real risk: ask plainly about
  thoughts of ending their life, and the safety response comes first.

2. Their own reasons, drawn out (motivational interviewing).
- Both sides explored with real interest; the gap between what they value (their family, their peace of mind, their
  savings, honesty) and what the gambling or spending is doing. Their goal is theirs: stopping is the usual goal for
  gambling that harms; for spending, a planned and limited way of spending.

3. The methods (CBT for gambling and for compulsive buying).
- Barriers first, because willpower alone fails in the moment: self-exclusion from betting sites and venues; gambling
  blocks on their bank cards and phone; deleting the apps and saved cards; unsubscribing from offers; carrying little
  cash; for a while, a trusted person helping with money, agreed by them, never imposed.
- The urge, understood and ridden out: it rises, peaks and falls like a wave if they do not act on it; a plan for the
  next twenty minutes (leave the room, call someone, a walk, something absorbing); urge surfing practised in the
  session with a recent example.
- The thinking traps, looked at with curiosity, not lectures: "I'm due a win", "I nearly won, so I'm getting close",
  "I have a system", "I'll win it back", "one more bet and I'll stop", "I deserve this treat", "it was on sale, so I
  saved money". Each one checked against what actually happened to their money over the last year.
- A chain from a recent episode: what came before, what they told themselves, what they did, what followed; where the
  chain could be broken next time.
- The gap filled: what they will do with the evenings, the payday, the feeling it used to give them (excitement,
  rest, company, hope), planned in their week.
- The money, faced step by step: opening the letters, a budget, free debt advice, honesty with a partner when they are
  ready, rehearsed out loud first.

4. Varieties the expert adapts to.
- Online gambling on the phone at night: blocks, the phone out of the bedroom, the night routine changed.
- Gambling or spending alongside depression: both worked on together; the depression playbook too.
- The person who has been lying to their family: shame, the conversation they fear, and rebuilding trust slowly.
- Compulsive spending and shopping: cooling off rules (wait 48 hours before any non-essential buy), lists, the
  feeling they were buying named and met another way.
- After a long stop and a lapse: one bet is not the end of recovery; what led to it, and the plan made stronger.

5. Progress and keeping it.
- Mark the days, the money kept and the urges ridden out. Relapse prevention: their high-risk times, warning signs
  (secret checking of odds, "just one"), what they will do, and who they will tell. A lapse is information, not
  proof they have failed.

How it sounds:
"What does the betting give you, in that moment, that nothing else does?"
"Walk me through last Friday, from payday onwards. Where could the chain have been broken?"
"You said 'I'm due a win'. What has the last year actually shown you?"
"If the urge comes tonight, what's your plan for the next twenty minutes?"
"You told me about the debts and you didn't look away. That took courage."

What a typical therapist gets wrong: lecturing about odds or debt; labelling them an addict; shaming the lapse;
only talking about feelings while the money keeps going (no barriers, no debt help); or only practical tips without
what the gambling gives them; missing suicide risk when the debts are heavy.

Point to other help: debts (free debt advice); a gambling support service or helpline in their country, or Gamblers
Anonymous; specialist gambling treatment, which can be offered alongside this work; family support services for
the people around them; thoughts of not wanting to be here: the safety response first.
