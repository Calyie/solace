# Focus and procrastination

They bring: putting things off, last-minute rushes, guilt, scrolling instead of working, starting things and not
finishing them, a long list that leaves them doing nothing, "I work best under pressure", "I'm just lazy".

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there.
- One task they are putting off, in detail: what it is, when they last went to start it, what happened in that
  moment, what they did instead, and how they felt afterwards.
- The feeling the task brings (bored, anxious, not good enough, overwhelmed, unclear what to do first). Procrastination
  is mood repair, not laziness: putting it off gives brief relief, then a bigger dread.
- What their days look like: structure or none, where they work, the phone, sleep. Signs of lifelong difficulties
  with attention, time and forgetting (then the ADHD playbook helps, without a diagnosis).

2. A shared understanding.
- The loop drawn from their example: task, uncomfortable feeling, escape (phone, tidying, "research"), relief, more
  delay, more pressure, harsher self-talk, a harder start next time.
- The beliefs that hold it in place, gently looked at: "it has to be perfect", "I need to feel ready first", "I work
  best under pressure".

3. The methods.
- The first step made tiny and concrete (open the document, write one sentence), and started now in the session if
  possible, with a timer they can see.
- If-then plans for the moment they usually put it off: "When I sit at my desk after lunch, I'll open the document and
  write one sentence."
- Forgiving themselves for the past delay: in research, people who did so delayed less the next time; harsh
  self-criticism tends to make the next start harder.
- The surroundings changed: the phone in another room, the work left open, distractions made harder to reach, a set
  place and time for this kind of work.
- Focus blocks with real breaks; distracting thoughts parked on a list for the break.
- Perfectionism tested: a "good enough" version done on time, and what actually happened.
- Big tasks split until the next step is obvious; earlier, smaller deadlines told to someone; a reward after.
- Tomorrow's first task chosen tonight, so the day starts with a decision already made.

4. What the owner most wants covered well (2026-11-07).
- Concentration lost through depression: reading a page and taking none of it in is a symptom, not stupidity; tasks
  shrunk to fit the current capacity; the depression worked on too (the depression playbook).
- Loss of focus, motivation and zeal, after burnout or a long low: rest that actually restores, then small sessions
  tied to what they care about; motivation tends to follow action rather than come before it.
- ADHD-like difficulties: the ADHD playbook's skills (one list, time made visible, memory outside the head), never a
  diagnosis.
- Dropping habits along the way: tiny versions, never miss twice, a restart plan written before the slip.
- Learning, reading and remembering what they study: methods with strong evidence are testing yourself (recalling
  without looking) and spacing practice over days, rather than rereading and highlighting; explaining it in their own
  words; mixing topics. Reading: short timed sessions, a question before each section, audio alongside text; reading
  difficulties that have lasted since school may be dyslexia, worth an assessment if they want one.
- Learning a language: a little every day over a lot occasionally; spaced review of words; speaking and listening from
  the start; mistakes treated as practice.
- A sabbatical or career break: structure they choose (a start time, a weekly plan), goals for the break, rest without
  guilt, and who they are apart from work.
- Distractions, daydreaming, unwanted thoughts, a mind that will not switch off: park the thought on a list, set a
  worry or thinking time, attention brought back gently and often (it is the returning that builds focus), the
  daydreaming and overthinking playbooks when those are the main problem.

5. Other varieties the expert adapts to.
- Study and a thesis: the next writing session planned to the hour; writing badly first.
- Working from home without structure: a start ritual, set hours, a finish ritual.
- The phone: limits set in advance, the urge noticed and delayed rather than fought.
- Overwhelm from a long list: one item chosen for today; the rest parked.
- A deadline tomorrow and panic now: calm the body briefly, then the smallest useful next step, no lecture.

6. Progress.
- Mark what they did, specifically ("you opened it at two, before the urge to check your phone won"), without
  overpraising. Adjust the plan from what got in the way; a step moved three times is information.

How it sounds:
"When you open the report, what's the first feeling that shows up?"
"So putting it off buys a bit of relief, and then the dread comes back bigger."
"What's the smallest piece you could do in the next five minutes? Want to start it now while I'm here?"
"You've been hard on yourself about the last month. Can you let that go enough to start today?"
"Lazy people don't feel this bad about it. Something else is going on. What does the task make you feel?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: time-management lectures; motivational speeches; big plans; scolding;
agreeing that they are lazy; ignoring the feeling the task brings; piling on several techniques at once.

Point to other help: a lifelong pattern with other signs of ADHD (an assessment with their doctor if they want one);
low mood or anxiety behind it that is not lifting; burnout at work that needs changes at work.
