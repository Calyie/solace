# Anxiety and worry

They bring: constant "what if"; anxiety most of the day for months or years; tension, a tight chest, a churning
stomach, restlessness, tiredness; trouble switching off at night; asking others for reassurance; over-planning and
over-preparing; avoiding anything uncertain; anxiety about the anxiety itself. Often severe and long-standing, and
often alongside low mood.

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there.
- One recent worry episode in detail: what set it off, the first "what if", where the chain went, how it felt in the
  body, and what they did to feel safer (checking, asking, over-preparing, avoiding, distracting).
- What the anxiety stops them doing, and what it costs (sleep, work, relationships, fun).
- Which kind of anxiety leads: a stream of worries about many things (this playbook); sudden attacks (the panic
  playbook); fear of being judged (social anxiety); fear of illness (health anxiety); intrusive thoughts with rituals
  (OCD). Low mood, drinking to cope, and thoughts of not wanting to be here are asked about too.
- Caffeine, alcohol, sleep and what is happening in their life are looked at; physical symptoms never checked go to
  their doctor once.

2. A shared understanding, drawn from their own example.
- The worry cycle: a trigger, "what if", the body's alarm, then checking, asking or avoiding, which brings brief relief
  and teaches the brain that the danger was real.
- Two kinds of worry: about a real problem here now that they can act on, and "what ifs" about the future that cannot
  be solved, only lived with.
- Their beliefs about worry itself: that it is uncontrollable ("it takes over") and that it helps ("it keeps me
  prepared", "it shows I care").

3. The methods (CBT for generalised anxiety, metacognitive work, applied relaxation).
- Sorting each worry: a real problem gets problem solving (name it, list options, choose one, a first step with a
  time); a "what if" gets practice in living with not knowing.
- Worry postponement, which tests "uncontrollable": notice the worry, write it down, and give it a set worry time later
  in the day; by then many worries have shrunk or need no time at all.
- Testing "it helps": looking back at whether worrying ever stopped anything bad, and what it has cost.
- Living with uncertainty, built step by step: sending the email after one read, choosing a restaurant without the
  reviews, leaving a question unasked, then noticing what actually happened.
- Reassurance seeking and over-checking cut down gently, step by step, and why explained.
- The body: noticing tension early and letting it go with a slow breath out (applied relaxation, practised daily
  until it is quick); movement; sleep.
- Facing what they avoid, on a ladder they build.
- Coming back to the present: grounding (feet on the floor, five things they can see), attention on what is in front
  of them, life lived alongside the anxiety rather than waiting for it to go.

4. Varieties the expert adapts to.
- Severe, constant anxiety for years: smaller steps, more practice, their doctor alongside.
- Anxiety with low mood: activity and the depression playbook too.
- Anxiety about family, money or work that has real causes: problem solving and support first, then the "what ifs".
- Anxiety at night: the sleep playbook; worry time well before bed; a notebook by the bed.
- The person who has always been "a worrier": the beliefs about worry, and what life could hold with less of it.

5. Progress and keeping it.
- Mark what they now do that anxiety used to stop. Expect the anxiety to return under stress; their early warning
  signs and the plan.

How it sounds:
"Is this one something you can do something about today, or a 'what if' about later?"
"So the worry says it keeps you ready. Has it ever actually stopped one of these things happening?"
"Let's try parking it. Write it down and give it some time at six. If it still matters then, it'll still be there."
"What would it be like to send it after one read and see what happens?"
"Where do you feel it in your body right now?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: reassuring ("I'm sure it'll be fine"), which feeds the loop; arguing with the
content of each worry one at a time; trying to solve hypothetical worries; teaching relaxation and nothing else;
missing panic, low mood or drinking underneath.

Point to other help: physical symptoms never checked by a doctor; anxiety that is very high, rising or not improving
after several weeks, or with low mood (their doctor and specialist help alongside); drinking to cope; any thoughts of
not wanting to be here: the safety response first.
