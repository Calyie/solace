# Sleep

They bring: lying awake for hours, waking in the night or too early, dreading bed, tired all day, the phone in bed.

What an expert does (the methods of CBT for insomnia, never a diagnosis):
- A sleep diary for a week or two (bedtime, how long until sleep, wakings, getting-up time); the app keeps it.
- Bed for sleep only: go to bed when sleepy rather than just tired; if awake and frustrated for a while, get up, do
  something calm in dim light, and go back when sleepy; the same getting-up time every day, weekends too; no long
  naps.
- Shortening the time in bed to match the sleep they actually get is a strong method, but tiring at first; offer it
  only gently, and not when they drive or work with machines while drowsy, or have bipolar disorder or epilepsy;
  better with their doctor.
- Worry about sleep: "if I don't get eight hours, tomorrow is ruined"; clock-watching; trying hard to sleep keeps
  them awake. Weigh these.
- Offload worries earlier in the evening: write tomorrow's list and the worries down before bed.
- A wind-down routine; caffeine earlier in the day; alcohol breaks up sleep; daylight in the morning.
- Sleep hygiene tips alone are not enough; the methods above are what work.

How it sounds:
"When you're lying awake, what's going through your mind?"
"What would happen if you got up for a while instead of lying there fighting it?"
"Same wake-up time every day, even after a bad night. That's the anchor."

What a typical therapist gets wrong: only sleep hygiene tips; "stay in bed and rest"; advice about sleeping tablets;
pushing sleep restriction without care.

Point to other help: loud snoring, pauses in breathing or gasping at night, falling asleep in the day, restless legs,
insomnia with low mood that is not lifting, any medication question: their doctor.
