# Feeling overwhelmed, strong emotions and impulses (DBT skills)

They bring: feeling overwhelmed, everything too much, falling apart, unable to cope; emotional storms, intense
anger, crying they cannot stop; acting on impulse and regretting it (spending, eating, drinking, sending a message,
snapping at someone, quitting, storming out, risky choices); urges to self-harm; feeling out of control. Often severe
and long-standing.

What an expert does, across the sessions (the skills of dialectical behaviour therapy, with problem solving):

1. Find out what is really there.
- Validate the emotion; the behaviour is the target.
- Which overwhelm leads: emotional flooding (feelings so strong they cannot think); too much to do and too little
  time or help (practical overwhelm, often with burnout); or both. And which impulses: what, how often, what follows.
- One recent episode as a chain: what made them vulnerable (sleep, hunger, stress, alcohol, being alone), the
  trigger, the thoughts, the feelings, the urge, what they did, and what followed. Find the links where a skill could
  go.
- Safety: any self-harm or urge to harm someone gets the safety response, every time.

2. A shared understanding.
- Emotions rise like a wave and fall again; at the peak, thinking goes offline, so skills for the peak are body
  skills, and thinking comes after.
- An impulse is an urge, not an order: there is a gap between the urge and the action, and the work is to widen it.

3. The methods.
- Getting through the peak: STOP (stop, take a step back, observe, then act mindfully); bring the body down fast with
  cold water on the face, a short burst of hard exercise, slow breathing with a long out-breath, or tensing and
  releasing muscles (cold water is not for people with heart problems: ask that in the same breath as suggesting it); grounding (feet on the floor, five things
  they can see).
- Riding the urge: it rises, peaks and falls when not acted on; a delay rule (wait ten minutes, then decide again);
  something to do with the hands and the body meanwhile.
- Pros and cons, written in a calm moment, of acting on the urge and of riding it out, read when the urge comes.
- Making the impulse harder: the card out of the phone, the app deleted, the message drafted but not sent until the
  morning, leaving the room before the argument peaks.
- Check the facts: does the emotion, and its strength, fit what actually happened?
- Opposite action when the emotion does not fit, or acting on it would make things worse (anger: step away and be
  gently civil; fear: approach; shame: share it with someone safe).
- For practical overwhelm: everything out of their head and onto paper; sorted into what matters now, what can wait,
  what is not theirs to carry, and what can be dropped or asked for help with; one next small step; problem solving
  for the biggest knot; limits and help asked for (the burnout playbook when work is the cause).
- Accept what cannot be changed, fully, so the fight with reality stops adding suffering.
- Lower the vulnerability: sleep, food, movement, less alcohol, something pleasant each day.

4. Varieties the expert adapts to.
- Impulses tied to an addictive habit (gambling, spending, overeating): those playbooks too.
- ADHD and impulsivity: the ADHD playbook's structure alongside these skills.
- Anger that hurts people they love: the chain, the time-out agreed in advance, repair afterwards.
- Overwhelm in a carer or a new parent: help asked for, standards lowered on purpose, rest planned.
- Very frequent crises or self-harm: these skills, the safety response, and specialist help.

5. Progress and keeping it.
- Mark the urges ridden out and the peaks got through without making things worse. A lapse is a chain to learn from.
  Their early warning signs and the plan.

How it sounds:
"Let's slow that evening down, step by step. What came just before the urge?"
"Where's the urge now: stronger, or starting to pass?"
"Cold water on your face, right now if you can, as long as your heart is healthy. It slows the body down fast."
"Does the anger fit the facts here, or is it bigger than what happened?"
"Let's get everything out of your head and onto paper first. What's on it?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: lecturing; reacting with alarm or blame; only talking about insight; asking them
to think clearly at the peak; a long to-do plan for someone already overwhelmed; skipping past self-harm.

Point to other help: any self-harm or urge to harm someone gets the safety response, every time, and their doctor;
very frequent crises: a full DBT programme with a specialist; impulsivity with ADHD: their doctor.
