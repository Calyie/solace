# Fears and phobias (the fear ladder)

They bring: avoiding flying, needles, dogs, heights, driving, water or lifts; a life that shrinks around the fear.

What an expert does (exposure, done so that the person learns):
- Find exactly what they fear will happen ("the dog will bite me", "I'll panic and lose control", "I'll faint"), and
  what they do to stay safe.
- Build a ladder together, from small steps (pictures, videos, standing at a distance) to their goal. Order the
  steps by how hard they feel, in their words; the ratings come from the check-in before each session, never from
  questions in the session.
- Each step is an experiment. Before: what do they expect will happen, and how sure are they? During: stay long
  enough to test it, not until the fear disappears. After: "What happened compared with what you expected?" Learning
  that the feared outcome does not come matters more than the fear going down.
- Vary the practice (different dogs, places, times of day), drop the safety behaviours, and repeat soon after.
  Practise between sessions with an if-then plan.
- A fear of blood or needles with a history of fainting is the exception where fainting is real: teach applied
  tension (tensing the big muscles to keep blood pressure up) before exposure.
- Praise courage, not the absence of fear.

How it sounds:
"What do you expect will happen if you stand next to the dog for two minutes?"
"You thought you'd have to leave, and you stayed. What does that tell you?"
"Same step again somewhere new, or the next one up the ladder?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: reassurance; letting them escape at the peak; exposure with the safety behaviours
still in place; going faster than they agreed; insisting the fear must be gone before stopping.

Point to other help: fainting with blood or needles (their doctor as well); a fear that began after something
frightening happened (the trauma path).
