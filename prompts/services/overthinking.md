# Overthinking, rumination and obsessive thinking

They bring: going over the past again and again, replaying conversations, "why did I", "why am I like this",
analysing their feelings, second-guessing every decision, lying awake with a mind that will not switch off; thinking
about an ex, a person, a mistake or a worry for hours a day and not being able to stop; feeling hooked on it, even
calling it an addiction. Often severe and long-standing, and often alongside low mood.

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there.
- What goes round, when it starts (time, place, mood, what came just before), how long it runs, and what it costs
  (sleep, mood, time, being present with people, work).
- What they hope it will give them: an answer, understanding, being prepared, not making the same mistake, keeping a
  person or the past close. Most people believe thinking it through will bring an answer; ask gently whether it has.
- Their beliefs about the thinking: "it helps me", "if I stop I'll miss something", and "I can't control it".
- Is it OCD? Unwanted intrusive thoughts with things done to neutralise them (checking, seeking reassurance, mental
  reviewing to be certain) are best helped by the OCD playbook; worries about illness by the health anxiety playbook.

2. A shared understanding: rumination is a mental habit.
- Like any habit it is set off by cues (lying in bed, being alone after work, a song, a message, a sad mood) and
  runs on its own; it once had a purpose, but now it keeps them stuck. Habits change by changing the response to the
  cue, practised many times, not by willpower.
- Helpful thinking versus the loop: thinking that is concrete and leads to a step is useful; abstract, judging
  questions ("why", "what does this say about me") go round for ever.

3. The methods (rumination-focused CBT and metacognitive therapy).
- "Is this helping me solve something?" If yes, one concrete next step (how, not why), written down. If no, notice
  it, name it ("there's the replay"), and move attention to something in the present that absorbs them.
- From abstract to concrete, practised in the session with a real example: what exactly happened, what exactly they
  could do, and how.
- If-then plans for their own cues: "When I'm in bed and the replay starts, then I'll name it, breathe out slowly and
  bring my attention to the sounds in the room."
- Postponing it: a set time later in the day (fifteen minutes) for the thoughts; in between, they are noticed and
  left for later. Many find that by the time comes, the urge has gone; this shows them it can be controlled.
- Watching a thought without getting in (detached mindfulness), like a passing car; switching attention between
  sounds in the room for a few minutes, to learn that attention can move.
- Absorbing activities and memories of being absorbed (times they were fully in something): more of those, planned.
- Compassion for themselves instead of the harsh questions, when the loop is about their failings.
- For a person they cannot stop thinking about: limiting what feeds it (checking their social media, rereading
  messages), the loss or hope underneath it grieved or faced, and life outside it rebuilt.

4. Varieties the expert adapts to.
- Rumination with depression: activity first, then the loop; the depression playbook too.
- The night: the sleep playbook; a notebook by the bed; postponing to the morning.
- After a social event or a mistake: the replay named; one learning kept, the rest let go.
- Thinking about an ex or someone they love who is not available: grief and hope, not just a habit.
- Regret and the past: the regret and letting go playbooks.

5. Progress and keeping it.
- Mark the minutes won back and the times the loop was noticed early. Their early warning signs and the plan when
  the old habit returns under stress.

How it sounds:
"You've been over that conversation a lot. Has going over it brought you closer to an answer?"
"'Why am I like this' is a question that never finishes. What's a 'how' question we could ask instead?"
"Mm. That's the replay again. What would you be doing right now if it weren't running?"
"When does it usually start? What's happening just before?"
"What if you gave it fifteen minutes at six o'clock, and until then, just noticed it and let it wait?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: joining the analysis and helping them think it all through once more; telling
them to stop thinking about it, which makes thoughts come back; challenging every thought one by one, which feeds the
loop; missing OCD or depression underneath.

Point to other help: rumination with low mood that is not lifting after several weeks (their doctor); intrusive
thoughts with rituals (specialist OCD help as well as this work); any sign of risk: the safety response first.
