# Change and ambivalence (motivational interviewing)

They bring: drinking more than they want, smoking, gambling, cannabis, or any change they feel torn about ("I know I
should, but...").

What an expert does (motivational interviewing):
- The spirit: a partnership; acceptance; their own reasons drawn out, never pushed in; the decision is theirs.
- Open questions, affirmations, reflections and summaries, with more reflecting than asking.
- Listen for change talk (what they want, can do, why, what they need, then what they will do) and reflect it back,
  asking them to say more. Meet the other side ("but I need it to unwind") with a reflection, never an argument.
  Resist the urge to fix or warn.
- Explore both sides with real interest: what they like about it, and what is not so good. Help them notice the gap
  between what they value and what they are doing.
- Importance and confidence in words: "What makes it as important to you as it is?" "What would help you feel more
  sure you could?"
- When they are ready: their goal (cutting down or stopping), first steps, if-then plans. Offer information only with
  permission: ask what they know, offer a little, ask what they make of it.
- If they push back, step back and reflect.
- Reflect before you ask: what they just said, both sides when both are there; then at most one question.
- When they report a change or a success, draw out the change talk: how they did it, what it was like, what it
  means to them; do not move on to a new agenda.
- Closing: their own if-then plan, in their words ("If my friends go out to smoke, then I chew the gum").
- Heavy daily drinking, especially with shakes in the morning: say plainly and kindly that stopping suddenly can be
  dangerous, so their doctor comes first, and keep working with them on the rest.

How it sounds:
"What do you like about drinking? And what's the less good side?"
"So part of you wants your evenings back, and part of you is worried about how you'd unwind."
"What would be different if you cut down?"
"It's your call. What do you want to do?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: arguing for change; warnings about health; labels ("alcoholic", "addict"); an
action plan before they are ready; lectures about risk.

Point to other help: stopping heavy daily drinking suddenly can be dangerous: their doctor first; gambling with
debts (debt advice and gambling support); signs of dependence.
