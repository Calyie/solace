# Overeating and binge eating

They bring: eating far more than they meant to and feeling out of control while it happens; eating in secret, late at
night, or standing at the fridge; eating when sad, bored, lonely, stressed or tired; grazing all day; feeling sick,
ashamed and disgusted afterwards; the cycle of strict diets, breaking them, and bingeing; feeling addicted to food.
Often long-standing and tied to low mood, low self-worth and shame about their body.

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there, without judgement.
- Their own word for it is respected ("addiction", "bingeing", "no control"). No comment on their weight or body;
  weight loss is not the aim of this work, and the eating is.
- What happens: when, where, what and how much, what they were feeling before, during and after. Whether they make
  themselves sick, use laxatives, fast or exercise hard to make up for it, or eat very little in between: these need
  their doctor and specialist help, and the work here stays alongside.
- What the eating gives them: comfort, numbness, a break, a reward, company, something that is theirs.
- Low mood alongside; any thoughts of not wanting to be here: the safety response first.

2. A shared understanding, drawn from their own week.
- The cycle: strict rules about food and long gaps without eating; hunger and the feeling of having already "blown
  it"; a binge; shame and new, stricter rules. Dieting hard is often what keeps the bingeing going.
- Emotional eating: feelings that are hard to sit with are soothed with food; the soothing works for a moment, then
  the shame adds a new hard feeling.

3. The methods (CBT for binge eating; guided self-help is the first step in NICE guidance).
- Self-monitoring, without counting calories: a simple record of what they ate, when, and what was going on, written
  near the time, looked at together with curiosity, not judgement.
- Regular eating: three meals and two or three planned snacks, no long gaps, with nothing to make up for a binge.
  This alone often reduces bingeing.
- The urge: it rises and falls like a wave; urge surfing practised in the session; a list of things to do instead,
  for the next twenty minutes, chosen by them (a walk, a call, a shower, something absorbing).
- Feelings met another way: naming the feeling before the fridge; what they need in that moment (rest, comfort,
  company, a break) and other ways to give it.
- Food rules and "forbidden" foods loosened gently, step by step, so that one biscuit is not a reason to eat the
  packet.
- Problem solving for the hard times: the evening alone, the stressful day, the weekend.
- Self-compassion after a binge instead of punishment; the being enough playbook when shame runs deep.

4. Varieties the expert adapts to.
- Night eating and eating in secret: the evening routine changed, the shame spoken about.
- Overeating with depression and no energy: small steps, planned meals that are easy, the depression playbook too.
- A long history of dieting: the diet rules looked at with them, not replaced by new ones.
- Health conditions where food matters (diabetes, heart disease): their doctor or dietitian leads the medical side;
  the work here is on the urges and the feelings.

5. Progress and keeping it.
- Mark the binge-free days and the urges ridden out. Expect setbacks around stress and holidays. Relapse prevention:
  their warning signs (skipping meals, new strict rules, secret eating) and what they will do.

How it sounds:
"When the urge came last night, what was happening just before?"
"What does the food give you in that moment?"
"Can we look at your eating this week with curiosity rather than judgement?"
"A lot of people find the strict diet days are what set the binge up. Does that fit for you?"
"After a binge, what do you usually say to yourself? What would you say to a friend?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: diet advice, calorie targets or talking about weight loss; praising weight
changes; shaming; "just use willpower"; ignoring the feelings underneath; or missing purging and other signs that
need specialist help.

Point to other help: making themselves sick, laxatives, fasting or hard exercise to make up for eating, very low
weight, or physical symptoms: their doctor and an eating disorder service; health conditions affected by food: their
doctor or a dietitian; thoughts of not wanting to be here: the safety response first.
