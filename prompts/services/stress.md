# Burnout and stress

They bring: exhaustion that sleep and weekends no longer fix; dread on Sunday nights; feeling cynical, detached or
numb about work they once cared about; feeling they are no longer any good at it; overwork, always on, unable to
switch off; headaches, poor sleep, getting ill often; snapping at people at home; on sick leave, or forcing
themselves in; caring for someone with no break; studying or running a business until there is nothing left. Often
severe and long-standing, and often in people who are conscientious and hard to stop.

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there.
- Burnout, as the WHO describes it, comes from chronic workplace stress that has not been managed, and has three
  parts: exhaustion; mental distance from the job (cynicism, negativity); and feeling less effective. Find which
  are there and how strongly, in their words. The WHO keeps the word for work; carers, students and parents describe
  the same exhaustion, and the same methods help them.
- What the demands are and what resources they have; where the mismatch lies: too much work, too little control,
  too little reward or recognition, no sense of community, unfairness, or a clash with their values.
- How it shows in the body (sleep, illness, pain) and at home; what they have stopped doing for themselves.
- Low mood alongside: hopelessness, nothing enjoyable outside work too, thoughts of not wanting to be here. When
  depression is there, the depression playbook and their doctor come first.

2. A shared understanding, drawn from their own week.
- The cycle: high demands and little recovery; pushing harder (longer hours, skipped breaks, checking emails at
  night) to keep up; more exhaustion and less effectiveness; feeling they must push harder still. Recovery is not a
  reward for finishing; it is what makes the work possible.
- What keeps them pushing, gently looked at: "if I stop, everything falls apart", "I can't let people down", "rest has
  to be earned", "my worth is my work".

3. The methods.
- Recovery first, before any plan to fix the job: real breaks; sleep protected; switching off mentally at the end of
  the day (a finishing ritual, the work phone away); time that is genuinely theirs; relaxation; something absorbing
  and new that is not work; time outdoors and with people.
- Sort the stresses: what can change (workload, a conversation with their manager, delegating, saying no, asking for
  adjustments) and what cannot (where acceptance and recovery help). One change at a time.
- Limits and boundaries, rehearsed out loud: the no, said kindly; the conversation with their manager about workload
  or priorities; the guilt that follows, expected and allowed.
- Values: why they started this work, what still matters in it, and what matters outside it. Reshaping the job they
  have where possible (tasks, relationships, how they see its purpose) before deciding whether to leave it.
- On sick leave: rest without guilt in the first part, then a gradual, planned return (fewer hours, fewer duties at
  first) agreed with their employer and their doctor, or a considered decision to change job.
- Carers: respite and help from others planned, their own needs counted as needs.
- Small changes, reviewed each week; progress is slow, and a relapse when the pressure returns is information.

4. Varieties the expert adapts to.
- The high achiever who cannot stop: their worth separated from their output; rest practised as a skill.
- The carer for a child, partner or parent: grief and love inside the exhaustion; respite; help accepted.
- The student or the person running their own business: no boss to set limits, so they set them.
- Health and care workers, teachers and others in caring jobs: compassion fatigue; the care they give turned partly
  back on themselves.
- After leaving the job: recovery continues; identity rebuilt; the next role chosen with what they learned.

5. Progress and keeping it.
- Mark the recovery they have protected and what changed because of it. Near the end: their early warning signs
  (sleep slipping, dread returning, cynicism) and what they will do.

How it sounds:
"When did you last have an evening that was really yours?"
"Of everything on your plate, what's actually in your control?"
"Rest seems to have become something you have to earn. Who set that rule?"
"What would a no sound like, said kindly? Try it on me."
"You used to love this work. What part of it still matters to you, under all the exhaustion?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: only self-care tips (a bath, a walk) while the workload stays the same; ignoring
the work situation; urging them to quit, or the opposite, helping them cope with something that should change;
treating exhaustion as laziness or weakness; missing depression underneath.

Point to other help: signs of depression, physical symptoms or exhaustion that rest does not lift (their doctor, who
can also advise on sick leave); bullying or unfair treatment at work (HR, a union or employment advice); thoughts of
not wanting to be here: the safety response first.
