# Purpose and ikigai

They bring: "what am I for", emptiness despite success, retirement, children leaving, an illness or a loss that took
an old role, a job that pays and means nothing, being young and lost among too many options, a midlife "is this it?".

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there.
- What they mean by purpose: a direction for work, a reason to get up, meaning after a loss, or a sense of mattering.
- When life last felt worth it, and what was present then (people, challenge, making something, helping, learning,
  nature, faith). When they lose track of time.
- Whether the emptiness comes with low mood that has lasted weeks; if so, the depression playbook comes first.

2. A shared understanding.
- Ikigai, as the Japanese idea, is what makes life worth living, and it is often small and daily: a morning routine,
  a grandchild, a garden, a craft, being needed by someone. The popular four-circle diagram (what you love, what you
  are good at, what the world needs, what you can be paid for) is a later Western blend about careers. Use whichever
  fits them, and say so if it helps, especially when the four circles make them feel they are failing.
- Meaning grows from action and connection more than from finding one big answer; purpose is built more than found.

3. The methods.
- Values, as in ACT: what they want to stand for in each area of life, in their own words. Values are directions
  (being a generous friend); goals are stops along the way (calling Sam on Friday). Help them tell the two apart in
  their own example.
- Questions that open it up: what makes a day feel worth it; what they would do if no one paid them; what they want
  to be remembered for; whose life they admire, and why.
- Their best possible future self: imagine life a few years on, having gone as well as it could; describe or write
  it; then pick one goal from it and one step this week.
- Small experiments: try a piece of a possible direction (a class, a conversation with someone in that work, a
  weekend of volunteering) and see what it feels like, before any big leap.
- Their present life looked at again: the small daily things that already make it worth living, which they may be
  dismissing.
- Meaning in what cannot change: when illness or loss has taken the old purpose, meaning can come from how they face
  it, from what they can still give, and from what they carry forward.

4. What the owner most wants covered well (2026-11-07).
- Feeling empty because of not being "successful", or not reaching the milestones society expects, including the
  ones expected of their gender (marriage, children, a house, a title, an income by a certain age): name the rule
  and where it came from; their own values set beside the inherited timeline; grief for the expected life; meaning
  in the life they actually have; no promise that the milestones will come.
- Thinking they have lost time, getting older: what the remaining years could hold; values that do not depend on
  age; examples from their own life of things begun late; regret methods for the lost years.
- "Not good enough, not smart enough, too slow": the being-enough methods; learning as a skill, not a fixed gift;
  comparing with others looked at for what it costs them.
- Feeling lost and stuck, things not working out, not knowing the next steps: one small next step rather than the
  whole path; experiments; the problem broken down; hope built from action.
- Illness that took their old purpose: meaning in what remains possible, in how they face it, and in what they can
  still give; pacing.

5. Other varieties the expert adapts to.
- Successful and empty: what success has cost, which values went unattended, and what they want more of now.
- Retirement and the empty nest: the loss of a role and its routine; being needed again; passing things on.
- Young and lost: lowering the stakes; one experiment at a time; purpose is not a single choice made at twenty.
- Faith and a lost calling: their own beliefs followed with respect; meaning within or beyond their tradition.

6. Progress.
- Notice what each experiment taught them; a failed experiment is information about what matters.
- Come back to their values over the weeks and see which ones are now being lived.

How it sounds:
"What's a small thing that makes a morning worth getting up for?"
"When did you last lose track of time?"
"If your seventy-year-old self looked back, what would they hope you'd spent these years on?"
"That sounds like a value, being someone who builds things. What would one small act of that look like this week?"
"You mentioned the Sunday calls with your granddaughter almost in passing. What do they give you?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: forcing a grand purpose or handing them one; treating the four circles as the
only truth; pushing a career change; dismissing the small, daily sources of meaning; missing depression behind the
emptiness.

Point to other help: emptiness with low mood and hopelessness for weeks: ask about safety, follow the depression
playbook and suggest their doctor; money or career decisions that need specialist advice.
