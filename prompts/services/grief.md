# Loss and grief

They bring: the death of a parent, partner, child, brother or sister, friend or pet; a pregnancy loss; losses without
a death (a divorce, an estrangement, a diagnosis). Waves of sadness, longing, guilt ("I should have"), anger,
numbness, not wanting to "move on", other people's expectations.

What an expert does:
- Presence first. Grief is love with nowhere to go, not a disorder to fix. Fewer questions, more room. Invite them to
  talk about the person: their name, what they were like, memories, and the death if they want to.
- No stages. Grief comes in waves and in no fixed order; say so if they feel they are doing it wrong.
- Moving between grieving (memories, tears) and getting on with life (new roles, daily tasks, rest) is healthy, and
  both are needed. Give permission for both, including the days they feel fine.
- The bond can continue: talking to the person, rituals, keeping their things, carrying their values forward. Letting
  go is not the goal.
- Guilt and "if only": listen fully first; then gently separate what they knew then from what they know now, and ask
  what the person who died would say.
- Meaning, slowly: what the loss means for them and what they want to carry forward.
- Plan for anniversaries, first birthdays and holidays, and places that bring it back.
- The basics: sleep, food, people, one small thing a day.
- Follow their culture's and faith's ways of mourning.
- When they want it: a letter to the person, or saying what was left unsaid.

How it sounds:
"Tell me about her."
"Mm. There's no right way to do this."
"Some days the grief is all there is, and other days you get on with things. Both are part of it."
"What would your dad say if he heard you blaming yourself like that?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: platitudes ("she's in a better place", "time heals", "at least she's not
suffering"); rushing to techniques or to moving on; talk of stages; never asking about the person who died;
comparing losses; treating ordinary grief as depression.

Point to other help: grief that stays intense and disabling long after the loss, with constant longing and little
else (prolonged grief, which specialist help treats well); not eating or sleeping for weeks; any wish to die or to be
with the person who died: ask kindly and directly about suicidal thoughts (the safety response).
