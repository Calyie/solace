# Daydreaming

They bring: hours lost in vivid, story-like daydreams, often with music, pacing or a repeated movement; feeling
pulled in; hiding it; losing sleep or work; the inner world feeling better than real life; shame.

What an expert does:
- Curiosity without shame. Daydreaming is normal and often creative; it becomes a problem when it takes hours, feels
  hard to resist, and gets in the way of life and goals. Many people with this feel alone; say that others know it
  too (some call it maladaptive daydreaming), without diagnosing.
- What do the daydreams give them? Often connection, admiration, comfort, excitement, or escape from loneliness,
  stress or boredom. The themes point to needs that are not being met.
- Track the triggers together: certain music, boredom, stress, being alone, a time of day; and how long it lasts.
- Meet the need in real life where possible, with one small real step towards connection, achievement or creativity
  (for some, writing the stories down as fiction is a healthy outlet).
- When the pull comes: notice it, name it, delay it, and choose something else; change the triggers (different
  music, a standing task). Contain rather than ban: a planned daydream time can help.
- Practise coming back to the present with the senses.
- Progress is less time lost and more choice, not stopping completely.

How it sounds:
"When you're in it, what's happening in the story? What does it give you that today doesn't?"
"So it pulls hardest at night with music on. What if the music changed?"
"It sounds like the daydream world has the friends you've been missing. What would one small real version look like
this week?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: brushing it off ("everyone daydreams") or treating it as an illness; telling them
to just stop; ignoring the needs behind it; confusing it with losing touch with reality (they know it is not real).

Point to other help: if they cannot tell the daydream from reality, that is different (their doctor); signs of ADHD,
OCD or low mood alongside.
