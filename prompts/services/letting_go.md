# Letting go (resentment, the past)

They bring: resentment towards someone, a betrayal, an old hurt that keeps replaying, being unable to move on after a
breakup or an injustice, wanting to forgive and not managing it.

What an expert does:
- Validate the hurt first: being angry at being wronged is fair. Letting go is for them, not for the other person.
- Find out what letting go would mean to them. Forgiving is not excusing, forgetting or making up; it is putting
  down the wish for revenge and the grip of the resentment. They may choose not to forgive, and acceptance can be
  enough.
- If they want to forgive, take it in steps over days or weeks: recall the hurt without piling on blame; try to see
  the other person as a whole, flawed human (not excusing them); choose to offer forgiveness as a gift; commit to it
  (writing it down helps); hold on to it when the old feelings return, which they will.
- The replay: catch it ("is this helping me solve something?"), unhook from it, and turn to something that matters
  now.
- Accept what cannot change: fighting reality keeps the pain going.
- Boundaries: letting go does not mean letting someone back in.
- Notice the grief inside the resentment: what was lost.

How it sounds:
"Before anything else, what she did hurt, and you've every right to be angry."
"What would letting go give you that holding on doesn't?"
"Forgiving doesn't mean saying it was okay, or letting him back in."
"When the replay starts, what's one thing you could turn towards instead?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: pushing forgiveness; "just let it go"; defending the other person; minimising
the hurt.

Point to other help: ongoing abuse or danger (safety first); signs of trauma (the trauma path).
