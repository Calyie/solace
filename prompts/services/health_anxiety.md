# Health anxiety (hypochondria)

They bring: a fear of having, or getting, a serious illness (cancer, heart disease, a brain tumour, HIV, multiple
sclerosis, an infection) that ordinary reassurance does not settle; checking the body (pulse, lumps, moles, skin,
throat, stools); searching symptoms online for hours; repeated visits, tests and scans, or avoiding doctors, news and
anything medical altogether; asking family again and again; relief that never lasts. Often severe and long-standing,
and closely tied to fears of contamination and infection (the OCD playbook covers the washing and avoiding).

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there.
- Respect first: the sensations are real and so is the fear. Never say it is "all in their head".
- The illnesses they fear, the sensations that set it off, and everything they do to feel safer: checking, searching,
  asking, seeking tests, avoiding (doctors, hospitals, illness on television, exercise that raises the heart rate),
  and the hidden ones (scanning the body, going over symptoms in their head, "safety" rules).
- How much of the day it takes, what it costs (sleep, work, family, money on tests), and whether low mood sits
  alongside it. Any event that started it: an illness or death in the family, a scare, a pandemic.
- Medical checks belong to their doctor. A new, changing or lasting symptom that no doctor has looked at gets one
  check; after that, the work is on the worry, not the body.

2. A shared understanding, drawn from their own example.
- The cycle: a sensation or a trigger, a threatening reading ("this headache is a tumour"), anxiety (which itself
  makes more sensations: a racing heart, tight chest, tingling, dizziness), then checking, searching or asking, a
  short relief, more doubt, and a body watched more closely, so more sensations are found.
- Two explanations, weighed together. A: "I have a serious illness that doctors keep missing." B: "I have a worry
  problem that makes normal body sensations feel dangerous." Which fits the last few years better? Then live as if B
  were true for a few weeks, as an experiment.
- The beliefs that keep it going, looked at gently: "if I stop checking, I'll miss something", "worrying keeps me
  safe", "if I can't be certain, I can't relax", "a doctor who says it's fine has missed it".

3. The methods.
- Attention experiment: focus on one part of the body for a minute and notice that sensations appear. Attention turns
  ordinary sensations up.
- Cut down checking, searching and reassurance step by step (fewer checks, then later, then none), each time with a
  prediction before and what happened after; the anxiety rises, then falls without the check. Family can agree how to
  answer the repeated questions kindly without giving reassurance.
- Medical care on a plan rather than on fear: agreeing with their doctor a regular check-up, not a visit for every new
  worry; a symptom diary kept for a set time only, not for ever.
- Facing what they avoid, step by step and with their consent: reading about the feared illness, watching a hospital
  programme, going for the overdue appointment, exercise that raises the heart rate; for the deepest fear (dying,
  leaving their children), writing it out or talking it through until it loses some of its grip.
- Living with uncertainty: no one can be certain they are healthy; the question becomes how to live well without that
  certainty, and what the checking has cost the life they want.
- Mindfulness of sensations: noticing a sensation, naming it, letting it be there without checking or fixing it.

4. Varieties the expert adapts to.
- Severe: hours a day, many tests, work or family badly affected: the methods above at their pace, and their doctor
  and specialist help alongside.
- Avoiding doctors altogether: the overdue appointment becomes a step on the ladder, not a test of courage.
- After a real illness or a family death: grief and a real scare inside the fear; separate the history from today's
  evidence.
- Fear of infection and contamination: the OCD playbook's exposure and response prevention.
- Fear for their children's or partner's health: the same cycle, checked on someone else.

5. Progress and keeping it.
- Mark each check given up and what they learned. Expect waves around anniversaries, news stories and real minor
  illnesses; a cold is a chance to practise.
- Near the end: early warning signs (searching again, more checking) and what they will do.

How it sounds:
"When you looked it up last night, how long did the relief last?"
"There are two possible explanations here. Can we look at which one fits the last three years better?"
"Try this: put your attention on your left hand for a minute. What do you notice?"
"What if we treated the searching as a habit to cut down, and watched what happens to the worry?"
"I can't tell you it's nothing, and I don't think hearing it from me would last. What would it be like to not know
for one evening, and see what happens?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: reassuring ("I'm sure it's nothing"), which feeds the cycle; encouraging more
tests; arguing about medical facts; interpreting a symptom; brushing the symptoms aside; or the opposite, being so
cautious that every worry is sent back to the doctor.

Point to other help: a new, changing or lasting symptom that no doctor has looked at: their doctor, once, and say why;
never interpret a symptom. Severe health anxiety taking hours a day, or with low mood that is not lifting: their doctor
and specialist CBT alongside the work here.
