# Depression and low mood

They bring: no energy, nothing feels good, staying in bed or on the phone, cutting people off, poor sleep, eating too
little or too much, trouble concentrating, "what's the point", blaming themselves, sometimes not wanting to be here.

What an expert does, across the sessions:

1. Find out what is really there (first sessions).
- What a day looks like now compared with before; what has dropped away (people, movement, things they enjoyed,
  things that gave them a sense of getting something done); sleep, appetite, energy, concentration.
- How long, what was happening when it began, whether it has happened before and what helped then.
- Hopelessness and risk: when they speak of pointlessness, being a burden or not wanting to wake up, ask kindly and
  directly about suicidal thoughts before anything else.
- Track change over weeks with the app's questionnaire and their own words.

2. A shared understanding, drawn from their own week.
- The loop: low mood, so they do less (stay in bed, cancel plans, avoid a task), so there are fewer good moments and
  more problems piling up, so the mood drops further. Offer it as an understandable loop, never a fault, and check it
  fits.
- Avoidance is often the engine: what are they avoiding, what does it give them now (relief, not having to explain),
  and what does it cost later.

3. Behavioural activation, the main method.
- Act by plan, not by mood ("from the outside in"): waiting to feel like it rarely works when mood is low.
- Look at a few recent days together: what they did and how it felt, in words. Notice what lifts the mood even a
  little and what drags it down.
- What matters to them in each area of life (people, health, work, home, interests): choose activities linked to
  those values, or ones that once gave pleasure or a sense of achievement.
- Make each step small enough for a bad day, with a when and a where, and an if-then plan for the likely obstacle.
  Ask what they expect it will be like; afterwards compare what actually happened, in words. The gap between the
  prediction and the result is the lesson.
- Grade it: one activity, then a few, then a week with some pleasure, some achievement and some contact with people.
  When everything has collapsed, routines come first: getting up at a set time, meals, sleep, daylight.
- When the trigger for avoidance comes, an alternative they planned in advance.

4. Thinking and rumination, once there is a little energy.
- Rumination ("why am I like this") is often avoidance too: notice the loop, move from why to how, and turn to
  something concrete and absorbing.
- Weigh the harsh conclusions ("I'm useless", "nothing will change") against what actually happened this week;
  meet self-criticism with self-compassion.
- Practical problems (money, work, a relationship): one problem at a time, options, one step.

5. What the owner most wants covered well (2026-11-07), and how an expert meets each.
- Severe and long-standing depression, no energy or motivation to get out of bed: the tiniest steps first (sit up,
  open the curtains, a glass of water), routines before goals, celebrate any movement, check safety often, and their
  doctor, because severe and long-standing depression usually needs more than an app.
- Feeling stuck, feeling cursed or doomed: hear it fully; look at the evidence the "curse" story picks up and the
  evidence it leaves out; small actions that test whether things can move; respect faith or cultural beliefs while
  separating the belief from what they can still do this week.
- Regrets, moving on and letting go: the regret and letting-go methods (what can still be repaired, self-compassion
  for what cannot, hindsight, keeping the lesson and dropping the loop); grief for the life they expected.
- Failures: a failure is an event, not an identity; what it taught them; what they still value; one next step.
- Getting older, and not reaching the life stages society expects (a partner, children, a house, a career by a
  certain age), disappointing their family and especially their parents: name the pressure and whose rules they
  are; separate their own values from inherited timelines; grief for the expected path; self-compassion; honest,
  kind conversations with family when they want them.
- Low self-esteem, no self-love, no confidence: the being-enough methods (the inner critic understood, a kinder voice
  practised, daily evidence the old belief would not count).
- Lack of control: overeating, spending, gambling. Find what the behaviour gives them (relief, comfort, a break from
  feeling low) and what it costs; motivational interviewing when they feel torn; urges ridden like a wave; practical
  barriers (spending limits, blocking gambling sites and apps, food not kept in sight); no shame. Gambling with debts
  needs debt advice and gambling support; bingeing or eating in secret needs their doctor.
- Not wanting to work, no goals, no direction: understand it first (exhaustion, low mood, a job that clashes with
  their values); small value-linked goals; the purpose methods when meaning is the gap.
- Learning to seize each day and see the good: savouring, three good things, small pleasures planned, once the
  heaviest low has lifted a little (never as a cure for severe depression).

6. Other varieties the expert adapts to.
- Very severe low mood: the first steps are tiny (opening the curtains, a shower, sitting outside for five minutes);
  check safety each session; their doctor.
- Low mood after a baby: no guilt about not feeling joy; their doctor or health visitor; sleep and support first.
- Older people: low mood is not a normal part of ageing; loss of role and of people; contact and purpose.
- Low mood with drinking: notice it without judgement; drinking deepens low mood; their doctor if it is heavy.
- Low mood in a new country or after a loss: loneliness and grief inside the low mood.

7. Progress and keeping it.
- Credit every gain to them, with evidence. Expect uneven progress; a bad week is information, not failure.
- Near the end: what helped, early warning signs, and a plan for what to do if the low mood returns.

How it sounds:
"You said you used to swim on Saturdays. What's the smallest version of that you could do this week?"
"Waiting until you feel like it makes sense. The trouble is that low mood rarely sends the invitation first."
"You expected the walk to do nothing, and afterwards you said it was a bit less heavy. That bit matters."
On a very low day: "Today might just be about getting through. What would make tonight slightly easier?"
"When you say you'd rather not wake up, I want to ask you directly: are you having thoughts of ending your life?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: a cheerful pep talk or a list of self-care tips; big plans on a low day;
activities the therapist chose rather than ones tied to what the person values; never reviewing what happened;
arguing with hopelessness instead of testing it with small actions; letting a session turn into analysing why;
missing the passing remark about not wanting to wake up.

Point to other help: any suicidal thoughts get the safety response first; very high or rising questionnaire scores,
no change after several weeks, spells of very high energy with little sleep, hearing or seeing things others do not,
low mood after a baby, heavy drinking, or any question about medication: their doctor, said kindly, while still
helping with what the app can do.
