# Social anxiety

They bring: fear of being judged, of blushing, shaking or saying something stupid; rehearsing beforehand and replaying
afterwards; avoiding meetings, parties, phone calls, eating in front of others.

What an expert does (the cognitive therapy for social anxiety that the research supports best):
- Take one recent situation in detail: what they feared would happen, the picture they had of how they looked from
  outside, where their attention went (usually inward, monitoring themselves), and what they did to stay safe
  (rehearsing sentences, avoiding eye contact, gripping a glass, staying quiet, hiding a blush, over-preparing).
- The attention and safety behaviour experiment: try a conversation once in the old way (watching themselves, using
  the safety behaviours) and once with attention on the other person and the safety behaviours dropped, then compare
  how anxious they felt and how they think they came across. In the session the therapist can be the other person.
- Test the picture in their head against reality: how they actually come across (a recording, a friend's honest
  view) compared with how they felt they came across.
- Behavioural experiments out in life, small and agreed: pausing mid-sentence, asking a simple question, admitting
  they did not know something, to test whether people react as predicted.
- Stop the post-mortem: the replay afterwards picks out the bad moments and adds them up. Agree to notice it and let
  it go.
- Cut down the rehearsing before events, step by step.

How it sounds:
"In that meeting, where was your attention: on what Sam was saying, or on how you were coming across?"
"What did you do to make sure you didn't look nervous?"
"Here's an experiment for the next call: put your attention on her words and drop the script. Then tell me how it
went compared with last time."
"The replay after the party, does it show you the whole evening, or mostly the awkward minute?"

What a typical therapist gets wrong: reassuring ("nobody is judging you"); exposure while the safety behaviours stay,
so nothing is learned; confidence pep talks; teaching social skills to someone whose skills are fine.

Point to other help: very severe avoidance (rarely leaving home), or drinking to get through social events; low mood
that is not lifting.
